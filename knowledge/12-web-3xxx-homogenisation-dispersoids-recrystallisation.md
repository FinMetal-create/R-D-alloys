## 1. As-cast Mn supersaturation and solute measurement by conductivity/TEP

### 1.1 Mechanisms
- **Conductivity is the standard 3xxx microchemistry probe.**
  - Li & Arnberg used electrical conductivity with TEM and image analysis to follow dispersoid precipitation during heating and homogenisation. They found that the dispersoid volume fraction measured by TEM followed **the same trend** as the volume fraction calculated from conductivity [W12-02, abstract]. A conductivity–solute relation was therefore used and validated against TEM, but its coefficients were not retrieved (§8).
  - The NTNU Al–Mn–Fe–Si studies use conductivity as one of three main probes, with hardness and EBSD, to separate precipitation from RX [W12-06, abstract; W12-15, snippet only].
- **Cold work accelerates Mn precipitation from supersaturated solid solution.** This was reported for Al–1.34Mn and Al–1.29Mn–0.05Si solution-treated at 640 °C and then cold-rolled [W12-22, abstract]. The as-cast or retained supersaturation is therefore a *deformation-activated* precipitation potential. This links §1 directly to §3.
- **Twin-roll-cast (TRC) strip.** The search summary reports that a homogenisation anneal "relaxes the supersaturation of the thin Al–Mn strip and avoids the precipitation–recrystallization interaction" [W12-23, snippet only; attribution uncertain]. This is consistent with the higher supersaturation of rapidly solidified strip already recorded in [ch03 §5.1 → Pol06 p.102; Leh06 p.171].

The kᵢ per wt% are much larger for the transition metals (Mn, Fe) than for Si or Mg. The specific 3xxx calibration (Li & Arnberg type) must be taken from W12-01 or W12-02 before use.

### 1.3 Design levers (slugs)
- **(inference)** The as-cast Mn supersaturation is what drives the precipitation–RX conflict in §3, together with the Si needed to form α. Higher supersaturation means:
  - the anneal is more sensitive to heating rate;
  - pancake grains become more likely at batch-anneal heating rates;
  - the P texture is stronger, since P sharpens with higher initial Mn supersaturation [W12-13, abstract].
- **(inference)** Strip-cast or otherwise rapidly solidified feedstock needs a homogenisation (or high-T hot-band anneal) that deliberately lowers Mn_ss if fine equiaxed grains are wanted after the final anneal [W12-23, snippet only].
- Use conductivity on (a) the as-received strip, (b) after the final anneal, and (c) on interrupted-anneal samples. This shows whether Mn precipitated before or after RX, as done in [W12-06; W12-15]. The in-house version of this lever is already noted in [ch03 §5.2 → Ryen03 §3.4].

## 2. Homogenisation: constituents, dispersoid precipitation, coarsening

### 2.1 Mechanisms
- **Precipitation sequence on heating, DC AA3003/3103** [W12-02, abstract]:
  1. **α-Al(Mn,Fe)Si is the first dispersoid phase to precipitate on heating.**
  2. **Long rod-like and plate-like Al₆(Mn,Fe) dispersoids** precipitate at higher temperatures.
  3. During holding at **600 °C**, the **Al₆(Mn,Fe) dispersoids grow while the α-Al(Mn,Fe)Si dispersoids dissolve quickly.**
  - **(inference)** At a high soak temperature the dispersoid population shifts from many fine α to fewer, coarser Al₆(Mn,Fe). That raises f/r-limited grain sizes and lowers Zener drag. This is the opposite of the "fine α by slow heating" lever in [ch04 §2.4].
- **Controlling processes.** Dispersoid evolution during homogenisation is controlled by nucleation, growth, Ostwald ripening and **hetero-precipitation** [W12-03, abstract].
- **Soak-temperature trend.** Dispersoids formed during homogenisation become **fewer and coarser as the soak temperature rises** [W12-04, snippet, background statement].
- **Constituents versus dispersoids.**
  - Above about **530 °C**, the large constituent particles coarsened **at the expense of fine dispersoids** [W12-05, snippet only].
  - In AA3104, raising the second-step temperature from **560 to 580 °C** increased dispersoid size and pushed the **α-phase share of the intermetallics above 50 %** [W12-04, snippet]. This is a proxy for the Al₆(Mn,Fe) → α constituent transformation: it is temperature-activated and is clearly advanced at 580 °C.
- **Low-temperature practice to keep Mn in solution.** The NTNU model alloy Al–0.53Fe–0.39Mn–0.152Si was DC cast, then heated at **50 °C h⁻¹ to 450 °C, held 4 h and water-quenched** [W12-15, snippet]. **(inference)** This practice is chosen to keep substantial Mn in supersaturated solution together with a fine dispersoid population. It produces the strong concurrent-precipitation condition studied in §3.

**Consistency check (derived).** For section counts, f_A ≈ N_A·π·r²:
- 560 °C: 2.84 µm⁻² × π × (0.047 µm)² = 0.0197, against the reported 0.0199.

The W12-04 numbers are therefore internally consistent, and the unit is per nm². For W12-05, the same relation gives f ≈ 2.8 × π × 0.0525² ≈ **0.024**. That is indicative only: the section-plane areal fraction is taken as F_V, and the measured diameter is treated as a true diameter.

**Zener pressure and Zener-limited grain size of these populations (derived).** Equations and γ = 0.324 J m⁻² from [ch03 §6 → HH eq.4.24; Tab.4.2]; area fraction taken as F_V:

| Population | P_Z = 3F_Vγ/(2r) | D_Z = 4r/(3F_V) | D_lim ≈ 2r/F_V |
|---|---|---|---|
| AA3104 560 °C (r 47 nm, F 0.0199) | 0.21 MPa | 3.1 µm | 4.7 µm |
| AA3104 580 °C (r 79 nm, F 0.0313) | 0.19 MPa | 3.4 µm | 5.0 µm |

- **(inference)** These Zener-limited sizes, a few µm, are an order of magnitude **below** the tens-of-µm RX grain sizes the user targets, and below the coarse pancake grains reported under concurrent precipitation (§3). Pre-existing dispersoids therefore do not set the RX grain size through a Smith–Zener limit. They act by:
  - suppressing nucleation, i.e. subgrain growth to critical size, where pinning is near its maximum [ch03 §6];
  - adding retarding pressure to slow-moving fronts.
- Once RX is complete, the curvature pressure for 30 µm grains (3γ/D ≈ 0.03 MPa, derived) is far below P_Z. **Post-RX grain growth is therefore blocked**, and the final grain size ≈ the nucleation-controlled RX grain size. This agrees with the conclusion in [ch03 §6].

### 2.3 Practical homogenisation windows (as far as supported)

| Goal | Practice indicated by the web sources | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Maximum Zener pinning for hot rolling (canstock AA3104) | Lower second-step temperature (560 rather than 580 °C) gives smaller, more numerous dispersoids; the authors call this a near-ideal combination for limiting RX during hot rolling | [W12-04, snippet] |
| Coarse, sparse dispersoids and low Mn_ss, which favour fine equiaxed grains after cold rolling plus annealing | High-temperature homogenisation (constituent coarsening at the expense of dispersoids above ≈530 °C; at 600 °C α dispersoids dissolve and Al₆ coarsens) | [W12-05, snippet; W12-02, abstract; W12-08, abstract] |
| High Mn_ss and fine dispersoids, giving strong concurrent precipitation (coarse or pancake grains, strong P texture) | Low-temperature practice (example: 50 °C h⁻¹ to 450 °C, 4 h, water quench) | [W12-15, snippet; W12-06, abstract] |

### 2.4 Design levers (slugs)
- **(inference, supported at abstract level)** For **full RX and equiaxed tens-of-µm grains** after the final anneal, the homogenisation (or hot-band practice) should leave **coarse, sparse dispersoids and low Mn_ss**. That means a high soak temperature and adequate time, plus a slow cool or a lower second step to precipitate Mn onto existing particles. Fine, dense dispersoids are the condition that "strongly slows recrystallization kinetics and produces coarse, strongly elongated grains" [W12-05, abstract; W12-06, abstract; W12-08, abstract].
- **The canstock "low second step" practice is the wrong template for slugs** when equiaxed grains are wanted. It is optimised for Zener pinning during hot rolling [W12-04] (inference).
- Track the result by conductivity: a higher conductivity after homogenisation means lower Mn_ss [W12-02].

- **ch02 §10 item 1 (Al₆(Mn,Fe) → α kinetics): partly.** The direction and temperature activation are confirmed (α share > 50 % at 580 vs 560 °C [W12-04]; α forms first, Al₆ coarsens at 600 °C [W12-02]). **No rate constants.**
- **ch04 §0 item 3 and §12 item 2 (Mn dispersoid kinetics): partly.** First literature number densities, sizes and area fractions for 3xxx dispersoids [W12-04; W12-05], and the soak-temperature trend.

## 3. Concurrent precipitation and RX in cold-rolled 3xxx

### 3.1 Mechanisms (literature-supported, mostly abstract level)
- **Three dispersoid populations act on RX** [W12-11, abstract]:
  - (i) dispersoids present before annealing;
  - (ii) dispersoids that form on grain and subgrain boundaries **before RX starts**;
  - (iii) dispersoids that form **during RX**.
- **Mutual interaction** [W12-18, abstract]:
  - Precipitation retards RX by **suppressing nucleation** and **pinning grain-boundary migration**.
  - Conversely, an early finish of RX **delays precipitation by removing nucleation sites**.
  - Both RX and precipitation kinetics are promoted by **larger cold deformation**.
- **Where precipitation occurs.** Precipitates form on **subgrain boundaries** and retard RX [W12-14, abstract/snippet]. Precipitation before RX completes is accelerated by **high Mn in solid solution, large deformations and high temperature** [W12-09, abstract]. Cold work itself accelerates Mn precipitation [W12-22].
- **Outcome when precipitation precedes RX** (the user's "precipitation before RX gives coarse, elongated grains"):
  - "Strong concurrent precipitation suppresses nucleation and retards recrystallization, which finally leads to a **coarse and pan-cake shaped grain structure**" [W12-08, abstract].
  - At low temperature, concurrent precipitation retards RX and gives **large flat grains**; grain size falls noticeably as annealing temperature rises [W12-19, abstract].
  - A fine dispersion of pre-existing dispersoids plus strong concurrent precipitation "may slow recrystallization considerably", giving a **very coarse grain structure** [W12-06, abstract]. Many small pre-existing dispersoids give a **significant static Zener drag** [W12-07, abstract].
  - Precipitation before RX gives strong resistance to RX in supersaturated Al–1.3Mn. This is the citing paper's paraphrase of [W12-20] (snippet only).
- **Outcome when RX precedes precipitation, or precipitation is weak.** "A refined grain structure with medium strength P and cube components is obtained when the pre-existing dispersoids are **coarser and fewer**, and concurrent precipitation is limited" [W12-08, abstract]. Similarly, "low number density and coarse pre-existing dispersoids, with limited concurrent precipitation, generally give a **fine, equiaxed** grain structure" [W12-05, abstract].
- **Heating rate (the batch vs CAL question)** [W12-10, abstract]:
  - **Faster heating gives a finer grain structure, a less elongated grain shape and lower flow stress**, in *both* the strong- and weak-precipitation conditions.
  - Under strong concurrent precipitation, a sharp P texture forms whose intensity **decreases** with heating rate.
  - Under weak precipitation, cube dominates and **strengthens** with heating rate.
  - Non-isothermal annealing gives **more inhomogeneous** microstructures than isothermal annealing, especially at **low heating rates** [W12-08 companion MSF paper, abstract; W12-09, abstract].
- **Multi-step schedules.** Compared with isothermal annealing, multi-step temperature–time schedules **raise the P component and lower cube and ND-rotated cube**. The **increase in P strength and in average grain size is largest when RX occurs concurrently with precipitation** [W12-11, abstract]. **(inference)** A low-temperature hold before the RX anneal is a *coarsening* tool, not a refining tool.
- **Pre-recovery.** Pre-recovery of a moderately deformed, supersaturated Al–Mn alloy, with annealing at 530 °C, gave **faster RX initially but sluggish RX at the later stage**. This was attributed to **precipitate-free zones** and loss of driving force during pre-recovery [W12-21, abstract].
- **Orientation dependence of drag** [W12-15, abstract; W12-17, abstract]:
  - Using a modified Smith–Zener expression for dispersoids on sub-boundaries, **cube subgrains experience the highest dispersoid drag** after recovery and partial RX, which cancels their usual size advantage.
  - The extra drag **favours PSN**. Fewer, larger dispersoids around large constituent particles lower the drag on PSN nuclei.
  - **P subgrains** form more often and grow faster than CubeND.

### 3.3 Design levers for slug alloy and annealing design
1. **Choose the sequence: RX first, then precipitation.** This is the literature-supported route to **fine, equiaxed grains** [W12-05; W12-08; W12-10]. Practical levers, all directionally supported:
   - **lower Mn_ss and coarser, fewer pre-existing dispersoids** before cold rolling (§2.4);
   - **faster heating** to the anneal temperature [W12-10];
   - a **higher anneal temperature**: grain size falls with T where precipitation competes [W12-19];
   - **higher cold reduction**, which promotes RX kinetics; it also promotes precipitation [W12-18], so it is not a stand-alone fix.
2. **In batch (coil) annealing at tens of °C h⁻¹,** a lean alloy (≈0.4 Mn, 0.15 Si) with high strain and Mn_ss lowered by a 450 °C/4 h treatment can still recrystallise fully by about 350 °C [W12-16, snippet only]. Slow ramps nevertheless give more inhomogeneous structures than isothermal anneals [W12-09; W12-08]. **(inference)** Expect a wider grain-size distribution in batch-annealed slugs than in fast-heated ones.
3. **Avoid low-temperature holds or pre-recovery steps before the RX anneal** if full RX and controlled grain size are the target. They strengthen P, coarsen grains [W12-11] and make the late stage sluggish [W12-21]. A hold below the RX start in the precipitation window is the recipe for pancake grains.
4. **(inference)** **Hardness.** Faster heating gave *lower* flow stress as well as finer grains [W12-10, abstract]. The abstract does not give the mechanism. Plausible contributors are less precipitation strengthening and a different texture or solute state, but this is not verified. Calibrate HB against heating rate with real slugs rather than inferring it from Hall–Petch alone.
5. **(inference)** Given P_Z ≈ 0.2 MPa (§2.2), the final grain size is set by the **number of successful nuclei**: PSN sites minus nucleation suppressed by boundary precipitation. A grain-size model for slugs should be **nucleation-density based** (N_PSN × survival probability), with the Zener cap applied only to post-RX growth.

## 4. RX kinetics parameters, PSN and texture

### 4.1 Mechanisms
- **JMAK exponent is not constant when precipitation competes.** n fell from 3.0 to 0.8 between 371 and 427 °C in a supersaturated Al–Mn alloy [W12-19, abstract]. Fitting a single n across the precipitation–RX crossover is therefore physically inappropriate (inference).
- **Non-isothermal JMAK fits depend on thermal history.** Applying classical JMAK directly to non-isothermal (DSC) data showed a dependence on thermal history [W12-24, abstract; Al–Mg–Si–Cu, not 3xxx]. **(inference)** A model that integrates isothermal JMAK along a batch-anneal T(t) path (additivity rule) will mis-predict when precipitation occurs during the ramp.
- **PSN in Fe/Mn-bearing alloys.**
  - PSN was promoted, and the RX temperature lowered, in alloys with **higher Fe and Mn** contents [W12-24, abstract; Al–Mg–Si–Cu].
  - Under concurrent precipitation, much of the P texture is attributed to PSN [W12-14, abstract].
  - Dispersoid drag on cube subgrains favours PSN, and dispersoid-depleted surroundings of large constituents help PSN nuclei [W12-15].
- **Texture as a diagnostic of the sequence.**
  - Strong concurrent precipitation gives a sharp **P {011}⟨566⟩** texture, with ND-rotated cube [W12-14; W12-10].
  - Weak precipitation gives **cube** dominance [W12-10].
  - P sharpens with **greater initial cold reduction, higher initial Mn supersaturation and lower annealing temperature** [W12-13, abstract via scite summary].
  - In non-isothermal annealing, P does not necessarily increase with Mn supersaturation, whereas **M texture** does [W12-08, abstract].
  - Sharp P and M textures are linked to RX at low temperature, where concurrent precipitation suppresses other nucleation routes [W12-12, one-sentence summary].
  - **(inference)** An EBSD texture check on slugs (P vs cube vs random) shows which side of the crossover the anneal was on.

### 4.3 Design levers (slugs)
- **(inference)** Use **at least two isothermal temperatures plus the real batch T(t)** for calibration. Do not extrapolate one Arrhenius line through the region where precipitation starts. The apparent Q can jump to several hundred kJ mol⁻¹ there [W12-19].
- **(inference)** For tens-of-µm equiaxed grains the PSN nucleus density must be high enough and not suppressed. Keep coarse constituents (the PSN sites [ch04 §2.5]) and avoid fine boundary precipitation before RX (§3.3).

### 4.4 Which gap this fills; Assessment against in-house models
- **ch03 §12 items 2 and 4; ch02 §10 item 3:** partly. There is one literature (n, Q) pair for supersaturated Al–Mn, and qualitative PSN/texture–precipitation links. **No d* or PSN-efficiency numbers.**
- **Assessment (ch09 §9 item 1).** The in-house RX models use single constant (n, Q) sets: n ≈ 1.8–3, Q ≈ 145–200 kJ mol⁻¹, as reproduced in ch09. The web literature indicates three things:
  - (a) under concurrent precipitation, n is **temperature dependent** (3.0 → 0.8 over 371–427 °C) [W12-19];
  - (b) the apparent Q can be **much higher** (≈456 kJ mol⁻¹) [W12-19];
  - (c) non-isothermal JMAK additivity fails when the thermal history changes the microstructure [W12-24].
  - **(inference)** None of the in-house parameter sets should be treated as transferable across Mn_ss levels or heating rates. A two-regime (RX-before vs precipitation-before) description, or an explicit P_Z(t) term, is needed. This critique is labelled here, and the in-house numbers in ch09 are left unchanged.
- **Assessment (ch09 §9 item 2; ch07 G12).**
  - The derived Zener limits for typical 3xxx dispersoid populations are about 3–5 µm (§2.2), below the measured or targeted tens-of-µm RX grains.
  - **(inference)** Grain-size predictions should therefore not be capped by a Zener limit at the RX stage. The RX grain size is nucleation-controlled, and the Zener term controls post-RX growth.

## 7. Cross-chapter conflicts and flags
- **Slow heating: helpful or harmful?** ch04 §0 item 3 and §2.4 recommend slow heating (50–75 °C h⁻¹) for a **fine, uniform dispersion** [R11 p.106]. That is right for *ingot* homogenisation when Zener pinning is wanted. For the **final anneal of cold-rolled slug strip**, slow heating is associated with coarser, more elongated grains [W12-10] and inhomogeneous structures [W12-09]. These are not contradictory. They apply to different process steps with opposite goals, and the KB should state the step explicitly whenever it cites "slow heating".
- **Sequence of dispersoid phases.** ch03 §5.1 says Si < 0.07 % gives Al₆(Mn,Fe) [HBAL03]. W12-02 reports that in AA3003/3103 (Si-bearing), α forms first on heating and Al₆(Mn,Fe) appears at higher T and coarsens at 600 °C. Both can hold; the phase depends on local Si and on temperature. Flagged for verification against the W12-01 full text.
- **Author attribution.** Authors are unconfirmed for W12-19 (Materials Letters 2010) and W12-14 (NTNU thesis). The W12-13 DOI match is unconfirmed. Verify before formal citation.
