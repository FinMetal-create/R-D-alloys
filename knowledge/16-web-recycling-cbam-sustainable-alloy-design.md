## 1. Recycling metallurgy: impurities, Fe and scrap-tolerant windows

### 1.1 Why impurities accumulate

- **Scrap volume and quality.** The amount of aluminium available for recycling is expected to roughly double by 2050. Much of it is post-consumer scrap with high levels of contaminating elements, and alloy design has to allow for this [W16-28].
- **Energy.** Remelting scrap needs only about 5 % of the energy of ore reduction [W16-28].
- **Raabe's research agenda.** The review covers how scrap-borne impurities affect precipitation (thermodynamics, kinetics, precipitate-free zones), casting microstructure and processing. Its goal is alloys with "the highest possible scrap fractions", including low-quality scrap [W16-28].
- **Problem elements.** Gaustad et al. list Si, Mg, Ni, Zn, Pb, Cr, Fe, Cu, V and Mn [W16-29].
  - Tramp elements are hard to remove from Al. Industry's most common remedy is dilution with primary metal [W16-29].
  - Many removal techniques exist, but most are difficult to retrofit into existing plants [W16-29].
- **Closed-loop can recycling** (Løvik & Müller, generic material-flow model) [W16-30]:
  - Fe, Ti and Pb can accumulate in closed loops [W16-30].
  - Raising the US can collection rate from about 54 % to 75 % could more than double impurity concentrations. This holds unless scrap treatment and remelting improve in parallel, or the scrap goes to other products [W16-30].
  - Higher collection rates take longer to reach steady state, and the steady-state concentration rises disproportionately [W16-30].
  - The authors had no actual impurity data for cans, because can-stock recipes are treated as trade secrets [W16-30].

### 1.2 Fe as the key problem: how Fe-rich intermetallics act

- **Nearly all Fe ends up in intermetallics.** The solid solubility of Fe in Al is ≤ 0.04–0.05 wt %, so in 1xxx/3xxx practically all Fe forms constituent particles [ch.02 §2.1].
- **Which phases form** depends on Fe/Si, Mn and cooling rate. The candidates are Al₃Fe, Al₆(Fe,Mn), α-Al₁₅(Fe,Mn)₃Si₂ / α-Al₈Fe₂Si and β-Al₅FeSi [ch.02 §1, §2].
  - β-Al₅FeSi is a hard monoclinic platelet (HV ≈ 5.8 GPa) [ch.02 §1].
  - Cubic α is the preferred form: it is compact ("Chinese script") and binds Fe, Si and Mn [ch.02 §9].
- **Fe/Si ratio and cooling rate** decide which Fe-phase transformations occur in recycled AlMgZn(Cu) crossover alloys [W16-32]:
  - One transformation helps rolling.
  - Another produces "hard-shell" particles that hinder processing [W16-32].
  - Fe and Si from recycled content form intermetallics that reduce formability [W16-32].
- **Primary (Fe,Mn)Al₆.** In 3xxx, coarse primary (Fe,Mn)Al₆ forms in the trough or sump when Fe + Mn exceeds about 2.0 % (3003) or 1.7 % (3004). Faster solidification raises these limits [ch.02 §2.7].
- **Why this matters for slugs** *(inference)*:
  - In backward impact extrusion of thin aerosol walls, coarse or plate-like constituents act as crack and tear initiators and as die-wear and surface-streak sources.
  - The constituent *size and shape*, not only the Fe content, sets the tolerable Fe.
  - That is why cooling rate (DC vs continuous casting) and homogenisation (Al₆(Mn,Fe) → α) matter as much as the chemistry.
  - No quantitative web data were retrieved linking Fe/Si content to slug formability, can-wall tearing or extrusion force (gap, §6).
- **Fe-tolerant alloy concepts found** (abstract level, none for packaging slugs):
  - **Al–Ce:** Al₁₀FeCe₂ forms and "neutralises" the harmful effect of high Fe, up to about 1 % Fe in recycled alloys. Si is to be kept controlled to avoid AlFeSi/AlCeSi phases that cost ductility [W16-34]. Ce is a rare earth (CRM issue, §4).
  - **High-throughput thermodynamic design** of an Al–Mg alloy family tolerating Fe and Si above 0.5 wt % with retained ductility; and an Al–Si casting alloy tolerant of high Mg and Fe, found by screening more than 300,000 chemistries with Scheil simulations [W16-35].
  - **Scandium:** Al₃(Sc,Ti) is studied for Fe/Si tolerance in a cross-recycled wrought alloy [W16-36]. Sc is a CRM (§4).

### 1.3 Mn/Fe ratio and Si control: "neutralising" Fe

- **Mn substitutes for Fe** in the Al₆(Mn,Fe) and α-Al₁₅(Fe,Mn)₃Si₂ lattices [ch.02 §2.4]:
  - Fe dissolves in Al₆Mn up to Al₁₂FeMn [ch.02 §2.4].
  - Al₃Fe can take up only about 4–5 % Mn [ch.02 §2.4].
  - Raising Mn at a given Fe therefore moves the constituent population from acicular Al₃Fe/β toward compact Al₆(Mn,Fe) and α [ch.02 §2.4–2.6] *(mechanism; no web quantification retrieved)*.
- **Mn:Fe rules of thumb.** A Mn:Fe ratio of about 0.5 is often quoted to convert β-Al₅FeSi to α in Al–Si *casting* alloys *(general knowledge, not verified this session; not established for wrought 3xxx)*. For lean 3xxx slugs the controlling limits are:
  - the Fe + Mn ceiling for primary (Fe,Mn)Al₆ [ch.02 §2.7];
  - the RX penalty of Mn in supersaturated solution and as α-dispersoids [ch.09 §9 item 3–5].
- **Keep Si low.** Si drives α-Al(Mn,Fe)Si dispersoid precipitation, and therefore Zener drag and slow, heating-rate-sensitive RX [ch.09 §9]. It also lowers the effective solidus and so the homogenisation ceiling [ch.02 §2.7]. The commercial scrap-based slug window below holds Si at 0.10–0.18 % [W16-33].
- **Fe/Si mass ratio as a phase selector:**
  - Al₈Fe₂Si (α) corresponds to Fe/Si ≈ 4.05 and Al₅FeSi (β) to ≈ 2.0 [ch.02 §2.2].
  - At high cooling rates Al₃Fe forms only when Fe:Si > 2 [ch.02 §2.2].
  - Crossover-alloy work confirms that Fe/Si ratio plus cooling rate select the transformation path [W16-32].

### 1.4 "Crossover" and "uni-alloy" concepts

- **Crossover alloys** (AlMgZn(Cu), "5/7 crossover") target the formability of 5xxx with the strength of 7xxx [W16-31, W16-32]:
  - They are designed to tolerate higher impurity levels [W16-32].
  - They avoid multi-material designs that hurt recyclability [W16-31].
  - Leoben/AMAG studied variants with different tramp-element contents and solidification rates, to check that they can be made from secondary raw materials [W16-32].
- **The "uni-alloy" (one-alloy-for-many-products) idea** was not found in the searches under that name (§6). For aerosol slugs the practical version is a **can-to-can (3104/3105 + 1xxx) blend** of the kind patented for impact-extruded containers (§1.5) [W16-33] *(inference)*.

### 1.6 Refining limits: what cannot be removed

- Removing tramp elements is challenging, and dilution with primary is the industry default [W16-29].
- Fe, Si, Cu, Mn, Ni, Cr, V and Ti cannot be removed economically in a conventional remelt shop. Mg, and partly Zn, Na, Ca, Li and H, can be lowered by fluxing, chlorination, degassing or distillation *(general knowledge; Gaustad et al. survey the options, but its full text was not read [W16-29])*.
- **Design consequence** *(inference)*: for a 100 %-scrap producer with **no primary to dilute with** (ch.09 §7), the alloy window *is* the scrap-mix mass balance. Fe, Si, Cu and Zn have to be tolerated by phase engineering (Mn, cooling rate, homogenisation) and by scrap sorting, not removed.

### 3.2 Scope for aluminium

- **Greenhouse gases.** Aluminium goods are listed against **CO₂ and perfluorocarbons (PFCs)** [W16-01].
- **Headings in Annex I** (per secondary list): 7601, 7603–7608, 7609 00 00, 7610, 7611 00 00, 7612, 7613 00 00, 7614, 7616 [W16-18].
  - The EUR-Lex snippets confirm 7612, 7613 00 00, 7614 and 7616 directly [W16-01].
- **Not covered:** 7602 (waste and scrap) and 7615 (table, kitchen and household articles) [W16-18].
- **Aerosol cans.** CN **7612 90 20**, "Containers of a kind used for aerosols, of aluminium", sits under heading 7612 [W16-17]. Imported aluminium aerosol cans are therefore CBAM goods [W16-01].
  - For the benchmark they fall in the "aluminium products" aggregated category (7612, 7613 00 00, 7614, 7616) [W16-02].
  - The reported end-of-chain add-on (+0.166) applies to containers [W16-08].
- **Slugs** (EN 570 product [W16-19]). No customs ruling was found:
  - A disc punched from strip is likely 7606 (plates/sheets/strip) or 7616 (other articles) *(general knowledge)*.
  - **Both headings are in Annex I** [W16-18], so imported slugs are very likely CBAM goods either way *(inference)*. Confirm with an EU Binding Tariff Information (BTI) search.

### 3.3 How embedded emissions are calculated

- **Direct emissions only.** Aluminium is an **Annex II good**, so from 2026 only *direct* emissions count; indirect electricity emissions are excluded [W16-01], [W16-03], [W16-04].
  - Cement and fertilisers are outside Annex II and count both [W16-04].
- **Precursors.** Complex goods carry the embedded emissions of their precursors. The guidance says this for iron and steel [W16-04].
  - For aluminium products the relevant precursor is unwrought aluminium (7601) *(inference; aluminium sector guidance not read)*.
- **Consequence** *(inference)*: electricity is about 70 % of the EU primary footprint [W16-23], and coal-based smelting reaches > 20 t CO₂e/t [W16-24]. CBAM prices only the anode/PFC/process part, a minority of the cradle-to-gate footprint.
  - European Aluminium wants indirect emissions to **stay out** of CBAM [W16-15].
- **Default values for CN 7601** (before mark-up):
  - India **1.87** t CO₂e/t [W16-07].
  - China **3** t (draft, per Argus) or **4.928** t (secondary page citing 2025/2621). **Conflicting, unverified** [W16-07].
  - No default for 7606 or 7612 was retrieved [W16-07].

### 3.6 Implications for an EU slug producer

All points below are *(inference)* from the facts cited.

1. **Feedstock.** Scrap (7602) is not a CBAM good [W16-18]. A 100 %-scrap slug producer that buys only scrap has **no direct CBAM obligation** on its feedstock.
   - The obligation starts once it imports more than 50 t/yr of Annex I goods [W16-05], for example unwrought 7601 ingot, master-alloy ingot classed as 7601, or semis.
   - At that point it needs authorised-declarant status [W16-06].
2. **Imported secondary ingot** made from post-consumer scrap currently carries zero scrap emissions [W16-14]. Only the remelter's own direct emissions count, and these are small (gate-to-gate remelting is about 0.5 t CO₂e/t in total [W16-21]).
   - From 2028, pre-consumer scrap content may become a precursor [W16-11], [W16-12]. Imported ingot rich in process scrap would then cost more.
3. **Competitors' imports.** Non-EU aerosol cans (7612 90 20) [W16-17] and very likely non-EU slugs (7606/7616) [W16-18] are CBAM goods.
   - Importers pay on the precursor's direct emissions above the benchmark [W16-08], [W16-10]. This gives EU slug and can makers some protection that grows to 2034.
   - The protection is weak against non-EU products made from post-consumer scrap (zero-rated) [W16-14].
4. **Price linkage (indirect exposure).** EU primary prices include the CBAM cost. Process-scrap prices track primary, so scrap is likely to "face a comparable carbon cost" [W16-14]. This matches the in-house deck's point that EU recyclers face higher scrap prices (ch.09 §7).
5. **Scrap availability.** EU aluminium-scrap exports were reported at 1.257 Mt in 2024 (+51 % since 2019) and 1.27 Mt in 2025 [W16-16].
   - A proposed export duty (15 % in some reports) was reportedly **withdrawn in Sept 2026**. Waste Shipment Regulation restrictions on exports to non-OECD countries are expected from 2027 (one analysis says May 2027) [W16-16].
   - European Aluminium had asked for a duty of at least 30 % [W16-16].
   - These are secondary reports of mixed reliability. They bear directly on the feedstock security of a 100 %-scrap route.
6. **Exports.** No search covered export-side CBAM relief for EU exporters of slugs or cans. Not verified (§6).

## 6. Relevance to in-house work (chapter 09)

- **ch.09 §7, "100 % scrap minimises CBAM exposure".** This is supported for *direct* CBAM obligations:
  - Scrap is not a CBAM good [W16-18].
  - Post-consumer scrap content is zero-rated in imports [W16-14].
  - The exposure is **indirect**, through EU primary and scrap price linkage [W16-14], and through scrap availability (export flows and WSR restrictions [W16-16]).
- **ch.09 §7, "$45/t (2026) → $308/t (2034)".** The deck's source is still unknown. With the reported 2026 inputs (India default, benchmark 1.423, €75–82 per t CO₂), Table 3.2 gives about €50–55 per t in 2026, close to the deck's first value. It gives €183–200 per t in 2034 at a constant price *(recalc.)*.
  - The deck's 2034 figure fits E = 2.431 t only if the carbon price is about 127 currency units per t CO₂ (308 / 2.431) *(recalc.)*. The deck uses $, while CBAM is priced in €.
  - The deck's assumptions still need to be obtained (ch.09 §11 Q10).
- **ch.09 §7 Assessment, "×40 ratio at constant carbon price".** This holds only for imports at benchmark-level intensity. For default-value imports it is about ×3.6, because the excess over the benchmark is charged in full from 2026 (§3.5, *recalc.*).

- **ch.09 §7 Assessment, "only direct emissions covered (ext., verify)".** Confirmed at snippet level [W16-01], [W16-03], [W16-04].
- **ch.09 §7 deck, "95 % energy savings vs primary".** Consistent with IAI and Raabe: about 95 % energy, 96.6 % GHG gate-to-gate excluding scrap burden [W16-21], [W16-28].
- **ch.09 §10 compositions vs the Ball patent window (Table 1.2):**
  - The AA3207 model input (Si 0.15, Fe 0.35, Mn 0.45, Mg 0.15, Cu 0.09, Ti 0.015) lies **inside** the US 12,385,112 claim-1 ranges for Si, Fe, Mn and Mg [W16-33].
